# Unit 2 — Minerals & Rocks

## What is a mineral?

A mineral is a naturally occurring, inorganic solid with a **definite chemical composition** and an **ordered atomic structure**. Four requirements:

1. Naturally occurring (not synthetic)
2. Inorganic
3. Solid
4. Definite composition + ordered structure

Quartz and feldspar are minerals. Rocks are **aggregates of one or more minerals** — granite is a rock made mostly of quartz, feldspar and mica.

## Identifying minerals by physical properties

| Property | How to test it |
| --- | --- |
| Hardness | Mohs scale 1–10; scratch tests |
| Cleavage | Tendency to split along flat planes |
| Fracture | How it breaks when it doesn't cleave (conchoidal, fibrous) |
| Luster | Metallic vs non-metallic shine |
| Streak | Colour of powder on an unglazed tile |
| Colour | Useful but least reliable |

> Rule: **streak beats colour**. Many minerals differ in colour but leave the same streak.

## The three rock families

- **Igneous**: formed from cooling magma or lava. Intrusive (granite) cools slowly below ground = coarse crystals; extrusive (basalt) cools fast at surface = fine crystals.
- **Sedimentary**: cemented fragments (sandstone, limestone), or chemical/biological deposits. Home of many fossils.
- **Metamorphic**: transformed by heat and pressure without melting (marble from limestone, schist from shale).

## Engineering significance

- **Igneous**: strong, reliable foundations; crushed for aggregates.
- **Sedimentary**: often weaker; limestone quarried for cement.
- **Metamorphic**: variable strength; marble and slate valued as building stone.

Always identify the rock *before* assuming its load-bearing behaviour.
